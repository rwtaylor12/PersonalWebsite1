AI Lab

Learning to build with AI

I'm early in my AI journey, and this page is where I show my work: the prompts I write, what came back, and what I learned along the way.

This whole website is an AI experiment. I built it in two evenings by describing what I wanted to Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, in plain English. I didn't write any of the code by hand. I made the decisions, gave the feedback and supplied the content. The build log below shows every step.

The process

How we built it

Every change followed the same loop.

1

I describe it

Plain English, like talking to a person: what I want, what I don't like, and what to keep. I attach things when I have them (my resume, photos, apps I'd built before).

2

The AI builds and tests

Claude Code writes the HTML, CSS and JavaScript, then opens the site in a real browser to check it on a computer and a phone, in light and dark mode, before showing me screenshots.

3

I react

I look at the screenshots or the live site and push back: "too basic," "the fish is cut off," "don't overdo it." Then we go around again.

4

It goes live

Each finished change is saved to GitHub, and GitHub Pages publishes it to this site a minute or two later. Every step is tracked, so nothing is lost.

What's under the hood

Claude Code (AI coding assistant) Plain HTML, CSS & JavaScript GitHub (version history) GitHub Pages (free hosting) FormSubmit (contact form email) Chart.js (tracker charts) Playwright (the AI's test browser)

Build log

The prompt trail

Every change to this site started with a prompt. Here they are, in order, in my own words.

v1 — The first prompt

The very first thing I typed into Claude Code:

I am wanting to build a personal website, a few different tabs/landing pages to show off my personality, projects, CV/resume, and show my (currently limited) ability to prompt/use/code with AI.

What came back: a four-page site (About, Projects, Resume, AI Lab) with a dark mode and placeholder text everywhere.

What I learned: a short prompt works, but the AI has to guess a lot (my name, my style, my projects). Every prompt after this one gave it more context.

v2 — Make it mine

The AI gave me a fill-in-the-blanks template. I filled in what I was ready to share and told it to skip the rest:

One-line intro: the road goes on forever, the party never ends --include hyperlink to the Robert Earl Keen Song on YouTube.
Short bio: I'm Ryan --> Business Faculty @ SAU Tech / Dad to Owen (4) & Reid (1) / Husband to Haley (Age N/A) / ...
Jobs: (lets just upload the resume later and skip this)
Colour/style preference: Blues & grays, modern/sleek style.

What came back: my real About page, a blue-and-gray theme and my name across the site.

What I learned: I paused the AI halfway through ("I need to keep working on that. Pause.") and it stopped cleanly. Finishing my notes and sending everything at once beat rushing.

v3 — "The most basic AI website ever"

I didn't like how it looked, so I said exactly why and described what I wanted instead:

I hate the tiles w/ emojis. The website currently looks like the most basic AI website ever. I'd like a rotating banner that I can customize with images that rotate behind my name & bio details. I don't mind tiles in a row below that banner that can take visitors to second pages. ... Make the "about me" section dynamic as well. Like a rolodex that spins through the details every few seconds.

What came back: the full-width rotating banner, the flipping "rolodex" of facts about me, and the row of page tiles. Every emoji gone.

What I learned: honest feedback beats polite feedback. Saying what I hated, what I liked and what to keep gave the AI a clear target.

v4 — Make it read like a sentence 
Let's take out the tabs right below the rolodex, since we already have larger tiles right below them. ... I want it to say "I'm Ryan --> (arrow) and then change the phrases to make syntaxial/grammatical sense. Don't overdo it.

What came back: "I'm Ryan →" followed by phrases that finish the sentence, like "dad to Owen (4) & Reid (1)." Later I rewrote all twelve phrases myself, word for word.

What I learned: "Don't overdo it" is a useful instruction. And sometimes it's fastest to write the words yourself and let the AI handle the layout.

v5 — A contact form and my real resume 
Let's work on the "Say Hello" tab. ... let it take you to a short contact form. If someone fills it out, have it send an email to [me]. Also, I have attached my resume. Under the Resume tab, make sure the actual resume can be viewed with one click. Allow people to download it as a PDF.

What came back: a contact form that emails me, and a resume page built from my PDF with one-click View and Download buttons.

What I learned: before publishing, the AI pointed out that my resume listed my home address and cell number, and asked what I wanted to do. The public copy has both removed. That's a privacy check I wouldn't have thought of.

v6 — Real photos, without taking over 
Here are some photos to scroll across the home page. Maybe make them semi-transparent? I don't want them to totally overtake the theme of the website.
Let's resize the photos so the fish, the kids, etc. are visible as well if possible.

What came back: my photos fading behind the banner at half strength, then each one shown whole with a soft, blurred copy filling the space around it.

What I learned: my iPhone photos had the GPS location of where they were taken hidden inside them. The AI stripped that out before posting. Also, describing the problem ("the fish is cut off") worked better than guessing at a fix.

v7 — Projects I'd already built 
Can you put this small workout tracker I built underneath the Projects tab? Enter some false data into it a few times so users can see how it works.
Add this Accounting I Chapter 4 Comprehensive Problem to the website under Projects. ... Feel free to change the theme/look to match my website.
Make my projects scroll automatically (like the pictures on the home page).

What came back: my 5×5 tracker loaded with nine made-up workouts, my accounting problem restyled to match the site, and a rotating showcase on the Projects page.

What I learned: apps I'd built in earlier AI chats dropped right in. The AI can pick up its own earlier work, restyle it and add sample data.

v8 — Owen & Reid, the easter egg 
Do you think we could build a little animation that randomly pops up around the website ... and asks if a visitor wants a quote, a riddle, a music/video suggestion, a photo ... Maybe make the animation a pair of cartoon dinosaurs named Owen and Reid? Owen should be orange, Reid should be blue.

Then, one improvement per prompt: a "Something else" button, exact YouTube links for my song picks, no videos, and "Here's two!" photos.

What came back: two cartoon dinosaurs who pop up now and then with a quote, riddle, song or photos. (Psst: type rawr on any page.)

What I learned: big creative ideas work best in layers. Get a basic version working, then add one thing at a time. And the AI won't guess what it can't know: it asked me for the exact YouTube links instead of making them up.

v9 — This page 
Update the info on the AI Lab page. Include new/updated info on how we've gone about building the site.

What came back: what you're reading now.

Rules of thumb

What I've learned so far

1. Context beats cleverness

Most bad results came from missing information, not bad wording. My resume, photos and real phrases did more than any fancy prompt.

2. Say what you hate, and what to keep

"I hate the tiles w/ emojis" plus "I like the resume and projects buttons" turned a generic site into mine.

3. Give it what it can't guess

Exact links, my own photos, my own words. The AI asked rather than inventing them, and the result was better for it.

4. Let it check privacy

Home address, phone number, photo locations: the AI flagged each before anything went public.

5. One change at a time

Small rounds made it easy to see what worked, and to undo what didn't.

6. Check the real thing

Look at the live site on your own phone. (And if it looks old, refresh. Browsers hang on to saved copies.)

Feedback

The honest redesign

Here's what I don't like about this: [be blunt]. Here's what I like and want to keep: [list]. Here's what I want instead: [describe it, with an example if you have one]. Don't overdo it.
Learning

Explain it back to me

Explain [topic] to me as if I'm smart but new to it. Use one concrete example. Then ask me 3 questions to check I understood, one at a time, and tell me what I got wrong.
Writing

Tough-love editor

You're a blunt editor. Point out the 3 weakest sentences in the text below and explain why. Don't rewrite anything yet — I want to fix it myself first.
Coding

Plan before code

Before writing any code, describe your plan in plain English: which files you'll change, what each change does, and any risks. Wait for my OK before you start.
